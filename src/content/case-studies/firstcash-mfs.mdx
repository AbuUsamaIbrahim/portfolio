## The problem

Mobile financial services in Bangladesh are, for a lot of people, the only banking they have. That
sets the bar: the system has to be correct about money in a way that most software never has to be.
There is no acceptable rounding error, no acceptable "eventually", and every movement has to be
explainable months later.

The platform had to hold customer balances, move money between wallets, let agents and distributors
transact on behalf of customers, disburse commissions, and settle at end of day — with a paper trail
good enough for a regulator.

## Shape of the system

<Diagram />

The centre of gravity is the **core ledger**: a Spring Boot service over PostgreSQL implementing
general-ledger semantics, integrated with **Apache Fineract** for the banking primitives. Every
product above it — the wallet, agent banking, education payments — is a domain layer that ultimately
expresses itself as journal entries. Nothing writes a balance directly.

## Agent banking: where the domain gets complicated

The interesting complexity was not the ledger. It was the distribution hierarchy sitting on top of
it. A DSR (distribution sales representative) transacts under a DO (distribution officer), who sits
under a distributor. Each level has its own transaction ceilings, its own visibility into the levels
below, and its own commission structure.

That produced work that looks mundane in a commit log and is not:

- **Transaction-limit validation** that resolves the acting role, walks to the correct ceiling, and
  refuses cleanly — before any ledger entry exists, not after.
- **Agent and limit-info listings** scoped so a DSR sees their own book and a DO sees theirs, without
  a query that accidentally leaks the whole hierarchy.
- **Distributor commission disbursement**, which is money movement driven by aggregate performance
  and therefore has to be both correct and reproducible at reporting time.

## End-of-day, and why reversal is a design decision

Every cycle closes. End-of-day reporting against the core banking system is where errors surface,
and it is the reason transaction reversal is implemented as a **compensating entry** rather than a
correction in place.

The rule I held to: the ledger is append-only. If something was wrong, you write the opposite of it
and both facts remain visible. It costs you a slightly noisier table and buys you the ability to
answer "what actually happened here" with evidence rather than inference.

## Education payments, built on the same core

EPMS extended the platform into institutional collections: fee creation and configuration, bulk
student import, and academic-year dashboards showing paid against unpaid.

The bulk import is the piece worth calling out. Importing thousands of student records from a
spreadsheet produced by a school office is an exercise in handling data that is wrong in every
possible way. Rather than reject a file on the first bad row, the importer validates every row,
imports what is valid, and reports failures with reasons and a downloadable list — so the office
fixes ten rows instead of hunting through three thousand.
